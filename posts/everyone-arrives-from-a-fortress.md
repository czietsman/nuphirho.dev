Jaco Louw's paper, "Entering Philosophical Space from a Porous Philosophical Place", asks what disposition a Western-trained philosopher should bring into a conversation between traditions. His answer is the Pyrrhonian one: withholding assent, so that there is no fortress to defend.

My first reaction, from the abstract, was to push back. Nobody arrives without a fortress. The nomad carries one too, and it is harder to see because he believes he has none. By the second page Jaco had named that problem himself, and by the end his porous philosopher stays rooted in her place while letting others reshape it: "neither dissolution nor armour". My view moved as I read, which is what the paper describes.

Where I still land differently is how firmly to hold ground. A scientific mindset holds what it has earned. It stays open to re-examining it but is not easily convinced: the challenge has to do the work, and so does the person challenged. That is provisional assent with the burden on the challenger, closer to the Academic sceptics than to Sextus, who would call it dogmatism about the non-evident. Perhaps the way through is that the fortress is the method, not the conclusions. The Pyrrhonist also keeps a practice and no doctrines.

The beliefs hardest to release are the embedded ones, born of our own tradition, which we do not know we hold. Sextus had a tool for those. The tenth Mode sets one people's customs and beliefs against another's, and it only works through contact with the other, which fits Jaco's point that composure is reached in relation, not alone.

Calling out the Western-trained philosopher is fair, but the requirement applies to every participant. Jaco warns against casting anyone permanently as defender or contester. A meeting of minds needs the same openness from all sides.

To give it, you have to be adept enough in your own field to look at your thinking from outside: free as the nomad rather than tied down as the soldier, with your identity coming from the movement rather than the place.
