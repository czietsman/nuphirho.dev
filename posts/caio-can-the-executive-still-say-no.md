A reader of the series introduction suggested a test I had not thought of. Ask what happens when the executive and the AI-supported recommendation disagree. Can the executive say what would make them reject it, and are there real examples where they have?

I think that is the right place to look, because it shows whether authority is still exercised, which an org chart cannot. The override path existing matters. Whether people can use it normally matters more.

The EU AI Act points the same way, for high-risk systems only. Those are the systems it regulates this way, and I am using its wording as a standard to measure against, not as a rule about decisions in general. Article 14 says the people assigned to oversee such a system must be enabled to decide not to use it, or to disregard, override or reverse its output, and to intervene or stop it. It also says they must be enabled to stay aware of the tendency to rely too much on what the system produces, which it names automation bias.

My reading is that "enabled" means more than permitted. A path that exists on paper but is rare, burdensome or hard to justify in practice has not enabled anyone. That is my interpretation of the word, not a quotation of the Act.

Rare overriding is not a failure by itself. If the recommendations are good, overrides should be rare. Engineers already have a way to think about that case. Failure mode and effects analysis treats a failure that seldom happens but does serious harm as a priority, not a footnote. A bad recommendation that nobody overrides is that kind of failure. I do not know what a healthy rate looks like, and I do not know of research that says. So the number alone cannot settle it. The questions can: what would make you reject this, when did you last, and who can stop it and how quickly?

This is also why I think the Chief AI Officer should not take the decisions. As I read the federal mandate's ten duties, they are about advising, coordinating and governing, not owning the outcome. I see the role as servant leadership. It sets boundaries, supplies evidence, and helps people get started, so the executive who owns the outcome can own it with more capability. If the role absorbs the decisions instead, "who owns AI?" gets a neat answer on the org chart while ownership of the decision gets less clear in practice.
